This book is a primer for an engineer who has touched a neural network at least
once and is comfortable with the words \emph{token}, \emph{transformer}, and
\emph{loss function}, but who would like a complete picture of \emph{why} the
field looks the way it does in 2026: why every frontier lab is publishing a
paper about \emph{long context}, why \emph{sparse} and \emph{linear} attention
keep coming back, and what a startup like Subquadratic is actually claiming
when it says it has a \emph{subquadratic sparse attention} mechanism.\sidenote{%
The companion video lives in the same repository under \texttt{vid/scenes/};
this book is the long form of the same argument.}

The ambition is to start from first principles and end somewhere uncomfortable.
By the last chapter, you should be able to (a) write down on a napkin why
\(n^2\) is unavoidable for dense attention, (b) draw the 2$\times$2 frame that
positions every alternative architecture against it, (c) read a benchmark table
for a long-context language model and not be fooled by it, and (d) hold an
informed opinion about what could replace attention in five years.

\newthought{The road map.} Sixteen numbered chapters in one continuous arc
(no book-level ``parts''): background through self-attention in the opening chapter, then the quadratic-cost story, scaling, engineering
mitigations, subquadratic alternatives, the 2$\times$2 frame and SSA, then
open questions.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Background.} What an LM is in one equation. Tokens
(BPE, WordPiece, SentencePiece). And the pre-transformer history that almost
everyone has forgotten: n-grams, RNNs, LSTMs, GRUs, sequence-to-sequence,
and Bahdanau attention as a small bolt-on that ate the world.

\item \textbf{The transformer.} The Vaswani recipe in detail.
Self-attention from scratch, with the matrix shapes everyone gets wrong.
Why the cost is exactly \(\bigO(n^2 d)\) per head per layer.

\item \textbf{Scaling laws.} Kaplan's straight line on a log-log
plot. Chinchilla's correction. What scaling laws \emph{don't} say about
inference and context length, which is the entire reason this book exists.

\item \textbf{Living with \(\bigO(n^2)\).} FlashAttention, KV
caches, multi-query and grouped-query attention, paged caches, sliding
windows, RoPE/YaRN. Brilliant engineering that does not change the asymptote.

\item \textbf{Subquadratic alternatives.} Fixed-pattern sparse,
state-space models (Mamba), long convolutions (Hyena), kernel-feature linear
attention, hybrids (SAMBA, Jamba, Griffin). What each one wins, and what
each one gives up.

\item \textbf{The 2$\times$2 frame and SubQ.} The single picture
that ties the whole map together. Subquadratic Sparse Attention
(SSA) as one bet on the bottom-right quadrant. How to read SubQ's published
benchmarks honestly.

\item \textbf{Open questions and what comes next.} Selection
functions at frontier scale, inference economics, diffusion language models,
JEPA, and honest speculation.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{tl;dr (read this if you read nothing else).}
Language models are next-token predictors; training fits their parameters so
that real text looks likely under that factorisation. Transformers implement
the conditional \(p(x_t \mid x_{<t})\) with dense self-attention, which scores
every query against every key; \(\bigO(n^2)\) work per layer in the sequence
length \(n\). FlashAttention and the KV-cache tricks in Sections~\ref{ch:flashattention}--\ref{ch:kv-cache} shrink constants
and memory traffic dramatically, but they do \emph{not} remove the asymptote:
at million-token contexts the bill breaks budgets anyway. Everything in Sections~\ref{ch:sparse}--\ref{ch:hybrids}
is an attempt to change the curve itself: sliding windows, state-space models,
linear attention, and hybrids, each trading away something (usually routing by
position or lossy state). The organising picture is the routing\,\(\times\)\,scaling
quadrant in Section~\ref{ch:quadrant}. Subquadratic Sparse Attention is a bet
on the bottom-right cell (linear cost \emph{and} content-dependent routing),
with public benchmarks that deserve scrutiny like any vendor-reported numbers.
This book is about why long context was expensive, how engineers cope today,
and where the architecture might go next.
